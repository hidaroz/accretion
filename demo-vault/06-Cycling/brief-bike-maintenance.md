> TL;DR: Chain wear drives everything else. Measure it, replace it at 0.5% stretch, and the cassette and chainrings last several chains instead of one.

## Chain wear

A chain does not stretch. The bushings wear, each link gets fractionally longer, and the
chain no longer sits correctly in the cogs it drives.

A worn chain then reshapes the cassette teeth to match. Fit a new chain to that cassette and
it skips under load, because the teeth now fit the old chain's spacing. That is how a £25
part turns into a £150 one.

Measure with a chain checker. Replace at **0.5%** for 11- and 12-speed, 0.75% for older
wider chains. Checked every few hundred kilometres, this costs one cheap part regularly
instead of three expensive ones occasionally.

## Cleaning and lubrication

Degrease, dry, then lubricate. Lube on a dirty chain makes grinding paste.

- **Dry lube** — dusty conditions. Attracts less grit, washes off in rain.
- **Wet lube** — wet conditions. Stays put, collects dirt.
- **Wax** — cleanest and longest-lasting, most setup effort.

One drop per roller, spin the cranks a few turns, then wipe the outside thoroughly. Lube
belongs *inside* the rollers; anything on the outer plates is only there to catch sand.

## Indexing

If shifting hesitates in one direction only, it is cable tension. Barrel adjuster: turn it
out to increase tension (shifts to larger cogs more readily), in to reduce it. Quarter turns,
test between each.

If shifting is bad across the whole range and the tension is right, check the derailleur
hanger alignment. Hangers bend from small impacts and a bent one makes perfect indexing
impossible, since the derailleur is no longer parallel to the cassette.

## Intervals

Chain check every 300km. Clean and lube every 200km, or after any wet ride. Cassette every
2–3 chains. Cables annually, sooner if shifting feels vague.
